Carbon monoxide lawyer in Pennsylvania: what landlords must fit, and how long you have.

Pennsylvania’s Carbon Monoxide Alarm Standards Act requires alarms in apartment buildings with fuel-burning heat, a fireplace or an attached garage, checked each time a new tenant moves in.

The short answer

In brief

In Pennsylvania you generally have two years to file a personal injury or wrongful death claim for carbon monoxide poisoning. If a city, township, county or other government unit may be responsible, written notice is usually due within six months.

Owners of apartment buildings with a fuel-burning heater or appliance, a fireplace or an attached garage must install carbon monoxide alarms and make sure they work when each new tenant moves in.

Pennsylvania time limits

Injury: 2 yearsFrom the date of the poisoning, in most cases (42 Pa.C.S. § 5524).
Wrongful death: 2 yearsGenerally counted from the date of death (§ 5524).
Government unit: 6 monthsWritten notice of the claim (42 Pa.C.S. § 5522).
ExceptionsChildren and injuries discovered later can follow different rules. Have a lawyer confirm your date.

Pennsylvania’s carbon monoxide alarm law

The Carbon Monoxide Alarm Standards Act (Act 121 of 2013) sets two main duties.

  • Rental apartment buildings with a fossil-fuel heater or appliance, a fireplace or an attached garage: the owner must install an approved alarm in a central spot near the bedrooms and the heater or fireplace (35 P.S. § 7225).
  • At each new tenancy the owner must replace alarms that are missing or broken and make sure the batteries work.
  • During a tenancy the tenant looks after the alarm and its batteries; the duty returns to the owner when the unit is empty.
  • Home sales: sellers must say on the property disclosure statement whether carbon monoxide alarms are installed.

Cities and townships can set stricter rules, and many have. Willfully failing to install or maintain a required alarm is a summary offense with a fine of up to $50, which is small. The bigger exposure is a civil claim if someone is hurt.

What we see in Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania has some of the oldest housing in the country. Old gas and oil boilers, converted coal chimneys, row houses that share walls and flues, and furnaces in basements under rented rooms are frequent sources. Winter storms and power cuts add generators and cars run in garages.

Who may be responsible

Landlord or ownerSafe heating and venting, and the alarms the Act requires.
Heating contractors and chimney servicesInstalled, inspected or serviced the system that failed.
UtilitiesGas service, meters and response to reported leaks.
ManufacturersBoilers, heaters, generators or alarms that were defective.

Questions

My apartment had no carbon monoxide alarm. Was one required?

If the building has a fuel-burning heater or appliance, a fireplace or an attached garage, the owner very likely had to install one. Your city or township may require more.

The alarm was there but the battery was dead. Who is responsible?

The owner had to make sure it worked when you moved in. After that the tenant generally looks after the batteries, but the facts matter, so keep photos and any messages about the alarm.
