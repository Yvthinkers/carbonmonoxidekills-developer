Carbon monoxide lawyer in Texas: your deadline, and the alarm law Texas doesn’t have.

Texas has no statewide law requiring carbon monoxide alarms in homes or rentals. That does not stop a claim. It means the case is built on what the landlord, owner or contractor should have done.

The short answer

In brief

In Texas you generally have two years to file a personal injury claim for carbon monoxide poisoning, and two years from the date of death for a wrongful death claim. If a city, county or other government body may be responsible, written notice is usually due within six months, and some cities set a shorter limit.

Texas law does not require carbon monoxide alarms in most homes, but landlords, owners and contractors still have to keep heating and fuel-burning equipment safe. Most Texas cases are built on that duty.

Texas time limits

Injury: 2 yearsFrom the date of the poisoning, in most cases (Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.003).
Wrongful death: 2 yearsCounted from the date of death (§ 16.003(b)).
Government body: 6 monthsWritten notice of the claim under the Texas Tort Claims Act (§ 101.101). City charters can require notice sooner.
ExceptionsChildren and some other cases follow different rules. Have a lawyer confirm your exact date.

Carbon monoxide alarms in Texas

Texas requires landlords to install smoke alarms in rental homes (Property Code chapter 92), but there is no matching statewide rule for carbon monoxide alarms. Whether an alarm was required depends on where you live and when the building was built or renovated, because many Texas cities adopt building and fire codes that require carbon monoxide alarms in homes with fuel-burning appliances or an attached garage.

No alarm law does not mean no claim. The questions are whether the furnace, water heater, generator or vent was installed and maintained properly, and whether the people responsible knew or should have known about the danger.

No statewide alarm law. The duty to keep heating safe still applies.

What we see in Texas

Many Texas homes heat with gas and few have alarms, so poisonings rise with cold snaps and power cuts. During the February 2021 winter storm, fire departments in Harris County alone answered 475 carbon monoxide calls in a single week. Families ran cars in attached garages, brought grills and generators indoors, or heated rooms with gas ovens.

  • Apartments and rented homes with old furnaces, water heaters or blocked flues.
  • Portable generators used too close to the house during outages, sometimes with missing warnings.
  • Hotels and short-term rentals with pool heaters or boilers next to guest rooms.
  • Workplaces using propane forklifts, gas-powered tools or poorly vented heaters.

Who may be responsible

Landlord or ownerRepairs that affect health and safety, including heating and venting.
Heating and gas contractorsInstalled, serviced or “fixed” the equipment that failed.
ManufacturersGenerators, heaters and appliances that were defective or lacked clear warnings.
Hotels and businessesOwe guests and customers reasonably safe premises.

Questions

There is no carbon monoxide alarm law in Texas. Can I still bring a claim?

Often, yes. A claim usually rests on negligence: equipment that was badly installed, poorly maintained or defective, or a landlord who ignored a reported problem. A missing alarm can still matter if your city’s code required one.

How long do I have in Texas?

Usually two years from the poisoning, or two years from the date of death for a family. Claims involving a government body need written notice much sooner, usually within six months.
