Guide · Hotels & vacation rentals

Carbon monoxide poisoning in a hotel: who is responsible, and what to do next.

A hotel guest cannot see, smell or taste carbon monoxide. When it reaches a room, the people who installed, maintained and inspected the equipment can be held to account.

The short answer

In brief

If you were poisoned by carbon monoxide in a hotel or vacation rental, the owner, the operator, and the companies that installed or serviced the equipment may be responsible. Get medical care first, keep every record, and get advice early, because evidence is often repaired or removed within days.

How it happens

Most hotel incidents trace back to fuel-burning equipment placed close to guest rooms: pool and spa heaters, water heaters, boilers and laundry equipment. A cracked heat exchanger, a blocked vent or a disconnected flue can push exhaust into rooms above or beside it, often at night, when guests are asleep.

Vacation rentals add their own risks: older furnaces, gas fireplaces, attached garages and generators run during power cuts. Detector requirements for lodging differ from state to state, and some properties have none in the rooms at all.

Guests don’t choose the heater, the vent or the inspection schedule. That is why the duty sits with those who do.

Who may be liable

Responsibility depends on the facts. These are the parties most often examined.

Hotel ownerKeeps the building safe and fit for guests.
Management companyRuns day-to-day maintenance and inspections.
Installers & service contractorsFitted or serviced the heater, boiler or venting.
ManufacturersMade equipment that failed or lacked warnings.

What to keep

Evidence decides these cases. As soon as you are safe and have been seen by a doctor:

  • Medical records, including any carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) blood test and when it was taken.
  • The 911 call and fire department report, with the carbon monoxide readings they recorded.
  • Your booking, room number and receipts, and the names of staff you spoke to.
  • Photos and video of the room, any alarm, vents and nearby equipment rooms.
  • A symptom diary: when symptoms started, eased and returned.

Symptoms to watch

Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion and tiredness are the most common early signs, and they are easy to mistake for travel fatigue or food poisoning. A strong clue is when several people in the same room, or the same floor, feel unwell at once. Problems with memory, concentration or mood can appear weeks later.

Questions

Can I claim if nobody was taken to hospital?

Possibly. Many exposures are never diagnosed at the time. Records from a later doctor’s visit, witness accounts and inspection findings can still support a claim.

The hotel offered a refund. Should I accept it?

Be careful about signing anything before you understand your rights. A refund offer can come with a release of claims.

How long do I have to act?

Time limits depend on the state where it happened and can be short. Start a free review so the deadline can be checked.
