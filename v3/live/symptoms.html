Symptoms & CO levels

Carbon monoxide symptoms are easy to mistake for something else.

Headaches, tiredness, nausea, confusion. Each one is ordinary on its own. Together, and especially when they ease away from home, they can point to carbon monoxide.

By Rob Aiers, founderWe have over 25 years dealing with CO cases. We know what makes a case and what doesn’t.Updated 21 Oct 20268 min read

The instrument

A silent alarm is not a safe home.

Carbon monoxide is measured in parts per million. Alarms certified to UL 2034 sound only after exposure at these levels for a set time, so harmful long-term exposure can go on for months without a sound.

Symptoms

If you have symptoms now
  1. Get everyone into fresh air, including pets.
  2. Call 911 from outside.
  3. Tell the medical team you suspect carbon monoxide, so they test for it.
SeverityCommon signs
MildHeadache (the most common sign), dizziness, nausea, tiredness, shortness of breath on effort.
ModerateConfusion, blurred vision, vomiting, chest pain, fast heartbeat, clumsiness.
SevereCollapse, seizures, loss of consciousness, heart damage and death.

Clues that point to carbon monoxide: several people or pets in the same place feel ill at once; symptoms improve when you are away and return when you come back; they are worse in the morning or when the heating is on.

Delayed and long-term effects

Some people seem to recover, then develop problems days or weeks later: memory loss, poor concentration, personality and mood changes, movement problems or depression. Doctors call these delayed neurological sequelae. Long-term, low-level exposure can also cause lasting headaches, fatigue and cognitive problems.

Feeling better is not the same as being well. Follow-up care matters.

How it is tested

The key test is a blood carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) level, taken from a vein or with a CO-oximeter. Timing is everything: COHb falls quickly once a person breathes fresh air or oxygen, so a normal result hours later does not rule out poisoning. A standard fingertip pulse oximeter cannot tell carbon monoxide from oxygen and may read normal even in serious poisoning.

  • Non-smokers usually have a COHb below about 3%.
  • Smokers commonly have higher background levels, up to around 10%.
  • Readings must be interpreted alongside symptoms and the time since exposure.

How it is treated

The first treatment is high-flow 100% oxygen, which clears carbon monoxide from the blood much faster than ordinary air. In serious cases, including loss of consciousness, pregnancy, or neurological or heart symptoms, doctors may consider hyperbaric oxygen therapy in a pressurized chamber.
