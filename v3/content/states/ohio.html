The short answer

In brief

In Ohio you generally have two years to file a personal injury claim for carbon monoxide poisoning, and two years from the date of death for a wrongful death claim. Claims against a city, county, township or other political subdivision also generally have two years.

Ohio’s building code requires carbon monoxide alarms in homes built since January 1, 2013, and the Ohio Fire Code added carbon monoxide detection rules for some buildings at the end of 2017. Many cities set their own rules for rentals.

Ohio time limits

Injury: 2 yearsFrom the date of the poisoning, in most cases (Ohio Revised Code 2305.10).
Wrongful death: 2 yearsCounted from the date of death (R.C. 2125.02).
Political subdivision: 2 yearsClaims against cities, counties and townships (R.C. 2744.04).
ExceptionsChildren and some product claims follow different rules. Have a lawyer confirm your date.

Carbon monoxide alarms in Ohio

  • New homes: the Residential Code of Ohio has required carbon monoxide alarms since January 1, 2013. The edition before it had none.
  • Some existing buildings: the 2017 Ohio Fire Code (section 915) added carbon monoxide detection where certain carbon monoxide sources exist.
  • Rentals: there is no single statewide rule for existing rental homes. Cities such as Troy and Lakewood have their own.

Without a required alarm, a case usually rests on whether the furnace, boiler, water heater or vent was installed and maintained properly, and whether the owner knew or should have known about the problem.

What we see in Ohio

Older housing heated by gas furnaces and boilers, shared chimneys in doubles and apartment buildings, and winter storms that bring generators and cars into garages. Ohio also has many workplace exposures, from warehouses running fuel-powered forklifts to industrial sites.

Who may be responsible

Landlord or ownerSafe heating and venting, and any alarms your city requires.
Heating contractors and utilitiesInstalled, serviced or inspected the system that failed.
Employers and site ownersVentilation and equipment at work, alongside any workers’ compensation claim.
ManufacturersFurnaces, heaters, generators or alarms that were defective.

Questions

My rental had no carbon monoxide alarm. Was one required?

It depends on when the building was built and on your city. Ohio has no single statewide rule for older rentals, but many cities do. Either way, the landlord still had to keep the heating safe.

How long do I have in Ohio?

Usually two years from the poisoning, or two years from the date of death for a family.
